## Patterns — when you see X, do Y
### Intake — A consumer got a letter saying their credit dispute was 'verified as accurate'. Classify the legal claim type and name the controlling statute. Be brief.
Here's the classification:

**Claim type:** FCRA § 611 dispute/reinvestigation failure — specifically, a challenge to a CRA's "verified" result where the reinvestigation lacked a reasonable basis.

**Controlling statute:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681i (FCRA § 611) — the CRA must "conduct a reasonable reinvestigation to determine whether the disputed information is inaccurate" within 30 days [corpus: fcra-may2023-508].

**Key nuance:** "Verified as accurate" is the CRA's conclusion, not a shield. The consumer's attack is whether the reinvestigation met the "reasonable" standard. Two layers:

1. **§ 611(a)** — the reinvestigation itself must be reasonable (not conclusory).
2. **§ 611(e)** — if the consumer provides specific reasons for the dispute, the CRA must consider them and the reinvestigation must be reasonably designed to determine accuracy.

If the letter just says "verified" without explaining what was checked, that's the § 611(a)(6) notice deficiency the CFPB has repeatedly flagged [corpus: cfpb_supervisory_highlights].

**Damages exposure if willful:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681n — actual damages or $100–$1,000 statutory per violation, plus attorney's fees [corpus: pa_legal_defs].

No payment is promised — the claim is that the CRA failed its statutory reinvestigation duty, not that it owes money outright.

### Intake — A collector wrote 'this is an attempt to collect a debt' and demanded payment in 7 days. What statute governs, and what is the consumer's first move? Brief.
Governing statute: the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA), 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692–1692p [corpus]. The "this is an attempt to collect a debt" line is the mini-Miranda disclosure required by § 1692e(11) — it marks the sender as a "debt collector" subject to the Act. Regulation F, 12 C.F.R. Part 1006, implements it [corpus].

The 7-day demand is the problem. Under § 1692g(a), within 5 days of the initial communication the collector must give written notice of: the amount, the creditor's name, and a statement that the consumer has THIRTY days after receipt to dispute the debt, with the debt "assumed to be valid" only if not disputed within that window [corpus, quoted verbatim from § 809 of the Act]. A demand for payment in 7 days that overshadows or contradicts the 30-day dispute right is itself an FDCPA violation (the Jerman line of cases confirms § 1692g(a) governs the contents of these notices, and "bona fide error" is no shield for legal mistakes — Jerman v. Carlisle, 559 U.S. 573 (2010) [corpus]).

Consumer's first move: send a WRITTEN dispute + validation demand within the 30-day window (certified mail, keep a copy). Effect under § 1692g(b): upon receipt of the written dispute, the collector "shall cease collection of the debt" until it obtains and mails verification [corpus — CFPB supervision materials quoting § 809(b)]. Do not pay anything, do not promise payment, do not acknowledge the debt as valid — a dispute costs nothing and forces the collector to prove up.

Secondary notes:
- If the collector never sent a compliant validation notice at all, that is a § 1692g(a) violation; the 7-day demand may also violate § 1692e(5)/(10) (threatening action it can't take; deceptive means) [corpus].
- Remedy for violations: private action under § 1692k (actual damages, up to $1,000 statutory, fees) [corpus].
- Caveat [NOT IN CORPUS verified this turn]: § 1692a(6) — the Act reaches "debt collectors," not original creditors collecting their own debts; confirm the sender's capacity before relying on FDCPA. State analogs (e.g., PA's FCEUA, 73 P.S. § 2270.1 et seq.) may add remedies — I have not corpus-verified that cite this turn, so treat it as a lead, not a grounded citation.

### Research — What must a creditor provide after taking adverse action on a credit application, and in what time? Cite the statute.
Grounded answer — corpus confirmed, statute quoted below.

WHAT the creditor must provide, and WHEN

1. Notice of action taken — within 30 days [official: 15 U.S.C. § 1691(d)(1) (ECOA § 701(d))]
"Within thirty days (or such longer reasonable time as specified in regulations ... for any class of credit transaction) after receipt of a completed application for credit, a creditor shall notify the applicant of its action on the application."
[corpus] CFPB Supervisory Highlights in the corpus states the same rule: "a creditor must send an adverse action notice to an applicant within 30 days of receipt of a completed application," implemented by Regulation B, 12 CFR Part 1002 (see 12 CFR 1002.9(a)(1)).

2. Statement of SPECIFIC reasons [official: 15 U.S.C. § 1691(d)(2)-(3)]
An applicant against whom adverse action is taken is entitled to a statement of reasons. The creditor satisfies this by either:
   (A) giving the statement of reasons in writing as a matter of course; or
   (B) giving written notification of the adverse action that discloses (i) the applicant's right to a statement of reasons within 30 days after the creditor receives a request — the request must be made within 60 days of the notification — and (ii) the identity of the person/office from which the statement can be obtained.
Section 1691(d)(3): the statement meets the requirement "only if it contains the specific reasons for the adverse action taken." Boilerplate ("did not meet our internal standards") does not satisfy this — Reg B official interpretations to 12 CFR 1002.9(b)(2) require reasons that "relate to and accurately describe the factors actually considered."

3. Content of the written notice [official: 12 CFR 1002.9(a)(2)]
[corpus] The notice must be in writing and contain: the action taken; the creditor's name and address; the ECOA § 701(a) anti-discrimination notice; the name/address of the creditor's federal enforcement agency; and either the specific reasons or the disclosure of the right to obtain them.

4. If the adverse action was based in whole or in part on a consumer report — additional FCRA duties [official: 15 U.S.C. § 1681m(a)]
The person taking the action must also provide: notice of the adverse action; the name, address, and toll-free phone number of the consumer reporting agency that furnished the report; a statement that the CRA did not make the decision and cannot give reasons; the credit score used (if a score was used) with related score disclosures; and notice of the consumer's right to a free copy of the report within 60 days and to dispute its accuracy. No fixed statutory day-count in § 1681m(a) — it attaches at the time of the adverse action.

5. "Adverse action" definition [official: 15 U.S.C. § 1691(d)(6)]
"A refusal to grant credit in substantially the amount or on substantially the terms requested" — excluding refusals where the applicant is delinquent/in default on that account, or where credit is refused because additional credit would exceed a previously established limit.

Timing summary
- Action notice: 30 days from completed application — § 1691(d)(1); 12 CFR 1002.9(a)(1).
- Specific reasons: automatically in writing, OR within 30 days of a request made within 60 days of the notice — § 1691(d)(2).
- FCRA disclosures (if a consumer report was involved): with the adverse action — § 1681m(a).

### Teach — Explain to a 6th grader what to do when a bank denies their credit card application. Plain words, 4 short steps, name the law once.
Here it is, kid-simple, with the real law behind each step.

WHAT TO DO IF A BANK SAYS NO TO YOUR CREDIT CARD

Step 1 — Read the letter they must send you.
The bank isn't allowed to just say "no" and walk away. A law called the
Fair Credit Reporting Act says that if they said no because of your
credit report, they have to tell you that, and tell you which company
(credit bureau) gave them the report. [official: 15 U.S.C. § 1681m(a)]
(They also owe you the actual reasons for the no — like "not enough
income" — within 30 days. [official: 15 U.S.C. § 1691(d)(2)–(3)])

Step 2 — Get your free credit report.
Because you got turned down, you can ask that credit bureau for a free
copy of your report. You have 60 days to ask, and it costs nothing.
[official: 15 U.S.C. § 1681j(b)]

Step 3 — Look for mistakes.
Read the report slowly. Is your name right? Are those really your
accounts? Does it say you didn't pay something that you actually paid?
Mistakes happen, and mistakes can cause a "no."

Step 4 — Tell the bureau about any mistake, in writing.
If something is wrong, dispute it — that means you tell the credit
bureau it's wrong. They then have to check it, normally within 30 days,
and fix or delete anything they can't prove is right.
[official: 15 U.S.C. § 1681i(a)]

Then, once your report is fixed, you can apply again.

- I named the law once for the kid (FCRA); the "reasons within 30 days"
  right technically comes from a sister law (ECOA, § 1691), cited above
  so nothing is invented.
- No one is promised money here. Disputing gets errors checked and
  fixed — it does not guarantee approval or any payment.
