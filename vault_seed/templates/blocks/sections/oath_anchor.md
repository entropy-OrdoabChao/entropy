## I. THE COURT'S OATH OF OFFICE — A NON-WAIVABLE DUTY TO UPHOLD THE CONSTITUTION AND ADMINISTER JUSTICE

A judicial officer takes an oath that imposes binding obligations — not discretionary courtesy. Under **Article VI, Clause 3** of the United States Constitution:

> "The Senators and Representatives before mentioned, and the Members of the several State Legislatures, and all executive and judicial Officers, both of the United States and of the several States, shall be bound by Oath or Affirmation, to support this Constitution."

Under **28 U.S.C. § 453**, a United States Circuit Judge must "take the oath of office prescribed by law before entering on the duties of his office." The oath requires that the officer "will support the Constitution of the United States."

Under **28 U.S.C. § 452**, a United States District Judge "is vested with the power of a magistrate judge in the exercise of judicial office."

The oath is not a one-time formality. It is a **continuing obligation** — every ruling, every order, every silence on a material question is a moment of oath performance or breach. When a judge signs an order, the oath demands:

1. **Support of the Constitution** — the order must be grounded in the Constitution and the laws of the United States.
2. **Fairness** — the order must not deny a party the opportunity to present their case, and must not rely on evidence not in the record.
3. **Justice** — the order must not produce a result that is arbitrary, capricious, or contrary to binding law.

> **IF** the Court's order at Dkt {{latest_order.dkt_no}} relied on a factual finding contradicted by the docket itself, AND the Court's oath demands that judicial findings be grounded in the record before it, THEN the order constitutes a breach of the oath obligation and is subject to correction.

> **IF** the Court's order relied on authority not binding on this Court, AND the Court's oath demands that rulings be grounded in the controlling law, THEN the order constitutes a breach of the oath obligation and is subject to correction.

> **IF** the Court's order denied a material argument without addressing the binding authority on which the argument depended, AND the Court's oath demands that the judge administer justice by applying the law to the facts, THEN the order constitutes a breach of the oath obligation and is subject to correction.

The oath is not a weapon for the judge — it is a shield for the litigant. It exists to ensure that the judge serves the Constitution, not the judge's own preferences.
