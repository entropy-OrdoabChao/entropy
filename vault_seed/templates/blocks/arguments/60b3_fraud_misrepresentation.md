## Fraud/Misrepresentation Under FRCP 60(b)(3)

### A. The Standard — FRCP 60(b)(3)

FRCP 60(b)(3) permits relief when "the judgment or order is based on fraud, misrepresentation, or misconduct by an opposing party." Three elements must be shown:

1. **Fraud, misrepresentation, or misconduct** by an opposing party (not the court);
2. The fraud prevented the movant from fully presenting the case; and
3. The fraud was not discovered before judgment even with due diligence.

**Unlike (b)(4), (b)(3) has a ONE-YEAR LIMIT** under FRCP 60(c)(1). The motion must be filed within one year after the judgment or order. If the one-year clock has expired, this subdivision is unavailable and (b)(4) or (d)(3) must be used instead.

### B. Application — {{fraud_description}}

{{#if fraud_description}}
**The fraud:** {{fraud_description}}

The opposing party's fraud/misrepresentation directly caused the court to enter a judgment based on false factual findings. The judgment is not merely erroneous — it is the product of deception that prevented the plaintiff from presenting the true case.

**IF** the judgment or order is based on fraud, misrepresentation, or misconduct by an opposing party, AND the movant did not discover the fraud before judgment even with due diligence, THEN relief under FRCP 60(b)(3) is warranted.
{{/if}}

### C. (b)(3) Fraud vs. (d)(3) Fraud on the Court

Per *United States v. A & P Trucking Co.*, 338 U.S. 121 (1949), FRCP 60(b)(3) covers fraud directed at an opposing party or the court's fact-finding process, while FRCP 60(d)(3) covers fraud directed at the court itself — "serious misconduct that undermines the integrity of the judicial process." Where the fraud involves the court relying on a falsified or contradicted record, both (b)(3) and (d)(3) may apply.
