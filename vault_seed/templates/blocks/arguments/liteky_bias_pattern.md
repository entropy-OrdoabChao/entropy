## Pattern of Conclusory Denials Creates Appearance of Bias Under Liteky and 28 U.S.C. § 455(a)

Under **Liteky v. United States**, 510 U.S. 540, 548 (1994), judicial rulings and comments
during proceedings generally do NOT constitute bias under 28 U.S.C. § 455(b)(1) unless they
"display a deep-seated favoritism or antagonism that would make fair judgment impossible."
However, the **appearance** of bias under § 455(a) is an objective standard: whether a
reasonable person, knowing all the circumstances, would harbor doubts about the judge's
impartiality.

In **{{caption}}**, the record reveals a pattern of conclusory denials that, viewed
collectively, creates the appearance of bias:

1. **Repeated labeling of procedurally-proper filings as "frivolous" or "premature"**
   without any analysis, citation to authority, or consideration of controlling law.
   Each denial is a bare assertion — the opposite of reasoned adjudication.

2. **Granting opposing counsel relief on bare assertions** while denying plaintiff's
   motions that are supported by controlling authority, without any explanation for
   the differential treatment.

3. **Creating procedural catch-22s** — denying summary judgment as "premature" while
   simultaneously denying discovery motions, making it impossible for plaintiff to
   meet its burden.

4. **Citing non-binding authority as controlling** — the scorecard establishes that
   opposing counsel has repeatedly cited authority that does not bind this court
   ({{non_binding_citations}}). The court's acceptance of these citations without
   analysis, while denying plaintiff's motions, creates the appearance that the
   court is not applying the law neutrally.

The Supreme Court in **Caperton v. A.T. Massey Coal Co.**, 556 U.S. 868 (2009),
reaffirmed that the appearance of bias — independent of any showing of actual bias —
is sufficient to require recusal under § 455(a). The pattern here meets that threshold.

**This is not a claim of actual bias.** It is a claim that the **pattern of rulings**
— conclusory denials, selective grant of opposing relief, citation of non-binding
authority as controlling — creates an appearance of partiality that a reasonable
person would question. Under Liteky, this pattern is precisely the kind of
"deep-seated antagonism" that § 455(a) was designed to address.

Plaintiff respectfully requests recusal under 28 U.S.C. § 455(a) and, in the
alternative, files an affidavit of bias under 28 U.S.C. § 144.
