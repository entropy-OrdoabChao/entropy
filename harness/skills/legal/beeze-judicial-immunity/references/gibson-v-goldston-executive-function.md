# Gibson v. Goldston — Executive Function Exception

## Case Details
- **Citation:** Gibson v. Goldston, No. 22-1757 (4th Cir. 2023)
- **Court:** U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit
- **Date:** October 30, 2023
- **Holding:** Judicial immunity does not shield judges who step outside their judicial role into executive functions

## Facts
Former West Virginia family court judge Louise Goldston was presiding over a property dispute between Matthew Gibson and his ex-wife. Rather than deciding the issues in her courtroom, Goldston:
- Ordered the parties to reconvene at Gibson's home in Raleigh County, WV
- Forced her way into Gibson's home under threat of arrest
- Personally led a search party comprising Gibson's ex, the ex's lawyer, and a bailiff
- Looked for small items like pictures and DVDs
- Sat shoeless in Gibson's armchair, instructing the party on how to search
- Approved seizure of items belonging to Gibson's children and girlfriend
- Forbade Gibson from recording the incident

## Legal Claims
- Fourth Amendment — warrantless search and seizure
- Fourteenth Amendment — due process
- First Amendment — restrictions on recording
- Equal Protection Clause — "home visits" disadvantaging pro se litigants

## Immunity Question
- Judge Goldston moved for summary judgment on judicial immunity grounds
- District court denied her motion
- 4th Circuit affirmed

## 4th Circuit Reasoning
- Judicial immunity protects only judicial acts
- The search of the house and seizure of property were nonjudicial acts
- While Goldston might have had the authority to *order* a search, the proper authority to *conduct* the operation was the local sheriff's department
- "Judges do not do double duty as sheriffs"
- Just as "judges do not do double duty as jailers," so too they do not do double duty as sheriffs

## Outcome
- Immunity denied — case went forward
- Judge was censured and fined by West Virginia Supreme Court
- Later retired amid push in WV Legislature to impeach her
- Settlement reached (amount undisclosed)

## Significance
- Fifth time in 50 years a federal appeals court denied judicial immunity for exercising executive power
- Establishes that when a judge personally executes a search, directs law enforcement, or performs any function normally done by law enforcement officers, immunity does not apply
- Key precedent for the "executive function" exception to absolute judicial immunity
