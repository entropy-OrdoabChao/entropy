# Clear Absence of All Jurisdiction — Jurisdictional Exception

## Overview
The narrowest exception to absolute judicial immunity: when the judge has NO jurisdiction whatsoever over the subject matter or the parties.

## Key Precedent
**Stump v. Sparkman, 435 U.S. 349 (1978)** — The defining case. Judge Stump approved a petition for sterilization of a 15-year-old minor without a hearing, without notice, and without the minor's consent. The Supreme Court held immunity applied because the judge had subject matter jurisdiction over probate matters. The Court established that immunity is lost only when the judge acts in the "clear absence of all jurisdiction."

## How to Prove

### Subject Matter Jurisdiction
- Show the judge had no authority over the type of case or subject matter
- Example: a probate judge trying a felony (Mireles v. Waco dissent)
- Example: a state judge hearing a federal question without federal jurisdiction

### Personal Jurisdiction
- Show the judge had no authority over the parties
- Example: a judge in one district trying a case over parties with no connection to that district

## Critical Distinction
**PITFALL:** Don't confuse "excess of jurisdiction" with "clear absence of all jurisdiction."

- **Excess of jurisdiction** = judge had jurisdiction but acted beyond its scope → IMMUNITY APPLIES
- **Clear absence of all jurisdiction** = judge had NO jurisdiction at all → IMMUNITY DOES NOT APPLY

The Stump Court was explicit: even if the judge acted in excess of jurisdiction, even if the judge acted maliciously or corruptly, immunity still applies. The exception requires the judge to have NO jurisdiction whatsoever.

## When This Exception Might Apply
- A judge in a different jurisdiction than the case
- A judge who was not sworn in or appointed
- A judge who has been removed from office
- A judge who is acting outside the geographic scope of their authority
- A judge who is acting outside the subject matter scope of their authority (but only if the judge had NO authority over that subject matter, not just excess)

## See Also
- Gibson v. Goldston, 22-1757 (4th Cir. 2023) — executive function exception
- Forrester v. White, 484 U.S. 219 (1988) — administrative function exception
- Mireles v. Waco, 502 U.S. 9 (1991) — ordering police is a judicial act (immunity applies)
